📅 CALENDAR (U+1F4C5) is Unicode's shorthand for dates, deadlines, and scheduling — a torn calendar page pinned at the top. Look closely on most platforms and the date on it never changes: it's fixed at July 17, a design choice that traces back to a specific Apple product announcement and later became the reason an entire unofficial holiday exists. This page identifies the glyph itself — meaning, codepoint, and how to type it — then covers the July 17 backstory in full, including the platforms that don't follow it.

History & Context

Why the calendar emoji is frozen on July 17

📅 CALENDAR was added in Unicode 6.0 (2010), part of the original wave of Unicode emoji drawn from Japanese carrier sets. Apple's artwork for it — a torn calendar page — needed some date printed on it, and Apple picked July 17 as an in-house reference: on July 17, 2002, Apple announced iCal, its calendar app, at the Macworld Expo in New York, alongside Mac OS X 10.2 "Jaguar." The date on the emoji is a nod to the date the product it represents was born.

That in-house detail became a public holiday in 2014, when Emojipedia co-founder Jeremy Burge created World Emoji Day and picked July 17 specifically because it was already sitting on Apple's calendar emoji. Once the day caught on — brand campaigns, emoji rankings, and Unicode Consortium news now regularly cluster around it — several other vendors redesigned their own calendar emoji artwork to match July 17 too, so the date wouldn't look arbitrary or wrong to anyone comparing platforms.

One distinction worth being precise about: the July 17 date lives on the emoji character — a static image baked into the font, the same on every device running that font version. It's a different thing from the actual Calendar app icon on iOS and macOS, which reads the system clock and updates to show today's real date. Typing 📅 always pastes the frozen July 17 artwork; opening the Calendar app never does.

Platform Differences

Not every vendor picked July 17

Unicode only specifies that a "calendar" emoji must exist — it doesn't dictate what date the artwork shows. For years, several vendors used the same trick Apple did: printing a date meaningful to their own company history instead of July 17.
