The em dash (—, Unicode U+2014) is the long dash writers use to set off a sudden break, a dramatic pause, or a parenthetical aside — roughly doing the job of a comma, colon, or pair of parentheses. It's also the character at the center of a running argument about AI-written text, and the one most often mixed up with the shorter en dash and the plain keyboard hyphen.

History & Context

Where the em dash comes from

The em dash gets its name from old metal typesetting, where it measured roughly the width of a capital M — one "em" — making it the widest of the horizontal dashes. Writers have relied on it for centuries to signal an abrupt break, an interruption, or a dramatic aside. Emily Dickinson famously built her poems around it more than 150 years ago, deep enough into literary history that the em dash has a pedigree entirely unconnected to anything digital.

That pedigree became a talking point in 2024 and 2025, when the em dash was recast as a supposed "tell" for AI-generated text. A widely shared Reddit post in October 2024 flagged ChatGPT's fondness for the mark as a dead giveaway, reasoning that em dashes "aren't on a keyboard." The idea spread fast: a January 2025 LinkedIn post declared weird long dashes the unmistakable work of ChatGPT, a February 2025 tweet calling em dashes a shortcut for detecting AI drew more than 11,000 likes, and by March a Reddit thread asking "how did the em dash become the signature AI detection punctuation?" had collected over 1,400 upvotes. In November 2025, NPR ran a feature titled "Inside the unofficial movement to save the em dash — from A.I.," quoting a writing coach who complained that for AI "it's like it's the only piece of punctuation they've learned other than a period."

Human writers pushed back hard. A recurring complaint across LinkedIn, Reddit, and the press ran along the lines of "I've always used em dashes — AI copied me, not the other way around," and one writer profiled by NPR runs a Substack literally called "The Em Dash," invoking Dickinson as the original power user. OpenAI eventually addressed the criticism head-on: when GPT-5.1 shipped in November 2025 it followed "don't use em dashes" custom instructions far more reliably, prompting TechCrunch to report that the company had fixed ChatGPT's em dash problem. Even so, guides like Wikipedia's own "Signs of AI writing" essay caution that heavy em-dash use is only a hint when paired with other signals — never proof by itself.

Underneath the AI drama sits a much older source of confusion: the em dash (—, U+2014) is one of several near-identical horizontal marks. The plain hyphen-minus on every keyboard (-, U+002D) is a compromise character that joins compound words; the en dash (–, U+2013) means "to" or "through" in ranges like "Aug 1–31"; and the minus sign (−, U+2212) sits slightly higher so it lines up with the plus sign in equations. Spacing conventions differ too — AP style surrounds the em dash with spaces, a habit traced to narrow newspaper columns, while Chicago style closes it up tight — which is why the same sentence can look different depending on who edited it.
