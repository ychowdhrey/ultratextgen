What to Say for Diwali

The greetings people actually use — in Hindi script, romanized, and translated — plus safe English lines for any audience.

Short answer

For most situations, plain "Happy Diwali" is the safe, universal greeting — it works for any recipient, in any context. If you want a line in Hindi, शुभ दीपावली (Shubh Dīpāvalī) — "auspicious Diwali" — is the everyday greeting almost everyone recognizes, and it reads naturally whether you write it in Devanagari script or type it in Latin letters as "Shubh Deepavali." A slightly longer, more formal version is दीपावली की शुभकामनाएं (Dīpāvalī kī shubhkāmnāeṁ), "best wishes for Diwali."

Safest default: Happy Diwali · Hindi alternative: Shubh Deepavali (शुभ दीपावली)
The two everyone recognizes

The greetings you can't go wrong with

Happy Diwali is the plain-English equivalent of "Merry Christmas" — it works for anyone, at any point during the festival, regardless of whether they celebrate the Hindu, Jain, or Sikh traditions tied to the holiday.

शुभ दीपावली (Shubh Dīpāvalī) literally combines "shubh" (auspicious, blessed) with "Deepavali" (the festival's full name, meaning "row of lamps"). It's the everyday Hindi greeting — warm, widely understood, and appropriate for family, friends, or coworkers.

More specific greetings

A more formal Hindi line, and safe English alternatives

Beyond the everyday greeting, there's a slightly more formal Hindi phrase, plus a handful of English lines that lean into the festival's imagery without requiring any Hindi at all:

  • दीपावली की शुभकामनाएं (Dīpāvalī kī shubhkāmnāeṁ) — "best wishes for Diwali," a slightly more formal line suited to a card or a message to an elder.
  • Wishing You Light and Prosperity — a warmer, wish-style English line for family or close friends.
  • Happy Diwali to You and Your Family — addresses a household rather than one person.
  • May Your Diwali Sparkle and Shine — leans into the fireworks-and-lights theme for a caption or story.
  • Happy Deepavali — the alternate spelling common across Southeast Asia and South India; use it interchangeably with "Diwali."
No Hindi? No problem

What to say if you don't know any Hindi greetings

Plain English is completely appropriate here — Diwali is celebrated across many languages and communities, and "Happy Diwali" or "Wishing You Light and Prosperity" reads as warm and correct on their own. You don't need to reach for a Hindi phrase to make the greeting land; save that for when you specifically want to add a cultural, native-language touch.

Getting it right

Script, romanization, or plain English?

All of these forms work — the choice is about formality and audience, not correctness.

Do
  • Use plain English ("Happy Diwali") when you're unsure of your recipient's background
  • Use romanized Hindi (e.g. "Shubh Deepavali") freely for casual texts and captions
  • Use the native Devanagari script (शुभ दीपावली) for cards, banners, or more formal messages
  • Ask a Hindi-speaking friend to check spelling and tone before anything formal or public
Don't
  • Assume machine-translated tone marks or diacritics are always exactly right
  • Overthink it for a casual text — English or romanized Hindi both read naturally
  • Mix Diwali greetings with unrelated holiday phrasing (this isn't Christmas or Lunar New Year wording)
