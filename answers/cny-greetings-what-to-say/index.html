What to Say for Chinese New Year

The greetings people actually use — in Chinese script, romanized, and translated — plus which one fits which moment.

Short answer

For most situations, 新年快乐 (Xīnnián kuàilè) — "Happy New Year" — is the safe, universal greeting. If you want something a little more festive and specific to the season, 恭喜发财 (Gōngxǐ fācái) — "wishing you prosperity" — is the other phrase almost everyone recognizes, and it's the line traditionally said right before red envelopes change hands. Both work in a text message, a card, or spoken out loud, and both are commonly written in Pinyin (the romanized form) when typing in Chinese characters isn't convenient.

Safest default: Xin Nian Kuai Le (新年快乐) · Festive alternative: Gong Xi Fa Cai (恭喜发财)
The two everyone recognizes

The two greetings you can't go wrong with

新年快乐 (Xīnnián kuàilè) translates directly to "New Year happy" — it's the equivalent of a plain "Happy New Year" and works for anyone, in any context, at any point during the holiday period.

恭喜发财 (Gōngxǐ fācái) literally means "congratulations, may you become wealthy." It sounds more transactional in English than it is in practice — it's a warm, expected wish for prosperity and good fortune, said constantly during the holiday and especially before giving or receiving a red envelope (紅包).

More specific greetings

Greetings for health, family, and abundance

Beyond the two universal lines, there are a handful of greetings for specific wishes:

  • 万事如意 (Wànshì rúyì) — "may everything go as you wish," a broad good-luck wish for the year ahead.
  • 年年有余 (Niánnián yǒuyú) — "may there be abundance every year," a classic New Year's phrase that plays on a pun between "surplus" (余) and "fish" (鱼), which is why fish dishes appear at reunion dinners.
  • 身体健康 (Shēntǐ jiànkāng) — "wishing you good health," commonly said to older relatives.
  • 合家欢乐 (Héjiā huānlè) — "happiness for the whole family," used when addressing a household rather than one person.
  • 金玉满堂 (Jīnyù mǎntáng) — "may treasures fill your home," a more ornate wish for wealth, often seen on banners and decorations rather than said casually.
New Year's Eve

What to say on the night before

The reunion-dinner night itself has its own greeting, separate from New Year's Day: 除夕快乐 (Chúxì kuàilè), "Happy New Year's Eve." Save 新年快乐 for the New Year's Day message that follows.

Getting it right

Characters, Pinyin, or both?

All of these greetings work in either form — the choice is about formality, not correctness.

Do
  • Use Pinyin (e.g. "Xin Nian Kuai Le") freely for casual texts and captions
  • Use the native characters (新年快乐) for cards, banners, or formal messages
  • Match the greeting to the moment — Eve vs. New Year's Day
  • Ask a Chinese-speaking friend to check tones/characters before anything formal or public
Don't
  • Mix up 除夕快乐 (Eve) with 新年快乐 (New Year's Day)
  • Assume Google Translate tone marks are always exactly right — double check for anything formal
  • Overthink it for a casual text — Pinyin alone reads naturally to most native speakers
