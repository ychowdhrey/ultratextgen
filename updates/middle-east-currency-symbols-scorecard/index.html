Middle East Currency Symbols in Unicode: The Scorecard After 18.0

Unicode 18.0 gives the UAE dirham and Omani rial their own currency symbols, following the Saudi riyal a version earlier. That's 5 Middle East currencies with a dedicated Unicode sign now in hand — and at least 10 more still sharing a generic symbol or using none at all.

Unicode 18.0 5 of ~15 currencies covered Regional scorecard
What shipped

Two More Gulf Currencies Get Their Own Symbol

Unicode 18.0's beta, published May 27, 2026, gives the UAE dirham and Omani rial their own dedicated currency symbols — following the same path the Saudi riyal cleared a full Unicode version earlier, finalized in September 2025. Stack those three next to the region's older entries — the Israeli new shekel (1993) and Turkish lira (2012) — and five Middle East currencies now have, or will shortly have, a currency symbol that's exclusively theirs.

That's real progress, but it's a minority. Look at the rest of the region and the picture splits into two very different categories: currencies that share someone else's generic symbol, and currencies with no dedicated symbol proposal on record at all.

Why it matters here

Three Currencies, Three Different Clocks

Each row in that table is really its own story with its own timeline — we track them individually: the dirham sign and its dated status update, the Omani rial sign, and the Saudi riyal sign — already final, but still waiting on mainstream font support a full Unicode version later. Same standard, three different clocks.

What's next

Iran Already Tried Once — and Was Sent Back

Of the currencies without their own symbol, only one has a documented Unicode submission on record: a 2017 proposal for a distinct Iranian "Toman" sign, which the Unicode Technical Committee reviewed and sent back, asking for evidence of real-world usage before it would reconsider. No resubmission has surfaced since. None of the other seven "no symbol" currencies in this scorecard have a known active proposal — which likely means no central bank has designed and formally adopted a candidate symbol yet, the step that has to happen before Unicode can act. We'll update this scorecard as that changes, the same way we're already tracking the dirham, Omani rial, and Saudi riyal individually.

A currency symbol isn't Unicode's decision to make.
A central bank has to design one and ask first.

That's why this scorecard is so uneven — and why it's worth checking back on.
