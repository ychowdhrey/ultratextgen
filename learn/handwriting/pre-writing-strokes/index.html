Pre-Writing Strokes: The Lines Every Letter Is Built From

Every letter breaks down into a handful of strokes — vertical lines, curves, and diagonals. Practicing them inside real letters, not disconnected shapes, is what actually gets a child ready to write.

Handwriting · Chapter 2 of 9 Pre-writing ⏱ 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Nearly every letter is built from four strokes: vertical lines, horizontal lines, circles/curves, and diagonals.
  • Practice the stroke inside a real letter, not a disconnected shape — the same motor skill, with no extra step to transfer it into writing.
  • l, i, t practice the vertical line; o, c, e practice the circle and curve; v, w, x practice the diagonal.
  • A child who can draw these strokes confidently on request is ready for almost any letter.

Why strokes, not shapes

Occupational therapists group early mark-making into a small set of core strokes: a vertical line, a horizontal line, a circle, and a diagonal. Most generic "pre-writing" worksheets practice these as loose shapes — a circle, a square, a triangle — sitting on their own with no connection to letters. That works, but it leaves a gap: the child has to separately learn that a vertical line is also "the stroke inside l, i, and t."

The simpler path is to skip the disconnected shape entirely and practice the stroke through the letter it belongs to. Typing a short run of one letter — iiii, oooo, llll — into a tracing sheet builds exactly the same line or curve a shape worksheet would, except the child finishes the session already able to write a real letter instead of a shape that still needs translating.

The four strokes, and which letters use them

Every letter in the alphabet reduces to a combination of four strokes: a vertical line (l, i, t, L, I, T, and the stems of h, b, d, k), a horizontal line (the crossbar in t and f, the middle bar in e), a circle or curve (o, c, e, a, and the bowls of b, d, p, q), and a diagonal line (v, w, x, k, y, and the crossbars in A, K, and X). Learning the stroke through one of its simplest letters — the vertical line through l, the curve through o — carries straight over to every other letter that uses it.

How to practice it

Start with the vertical line (l, i, t) since it needs the least hand control, then move to the circle and curve (o, c, e). Keep sessions to a handful of repetitions at a large size — our bubble letters give an oversized single letter that's easy to finger-trace before a crayon ever touches paper. Once a child can draw the stroke steadily on request, move to the first real letters — at that point nearly every letter is just these four strokes in a new combination.
