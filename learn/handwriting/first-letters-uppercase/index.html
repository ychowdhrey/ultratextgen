First Letters: Why Uppercase Usually Comes First

Most handwriting programs teach capitals before lowercase — not because they're used more, but because they're simpler shapes and easier to tell apart. The case for each order, and how to pick.

Handwriting · Chapter 3 of 9 First letters ⏱ 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Capitals are simpler shapes — mostly straight lines and full circles — and easier to tell apart than lowercase.
  • Lowercase is what's actually used most in real writing and reading; it matters just as much, just usually second.
  • It's a real, ongoing debate: some programs start with capitals, others start with lowercase to match reading instruction.
  • Either order works if you're consistent — and the child's own name is the best starting point either way.

The case for uppercase first

Most well-known handwriting programs — Handwriting Without Tears among them — teach capital letters first. The reasoning is mechanical: capitals are built almost entirely from the strokes covered in the previous chapter — straight lines and full circles — with far fewer of the small curves and loops that make lowercase harder to control. Capitals are also more visually distinct from each other: there's no capital equivalent of the b/d/p/q confusion that trips up early lowercase writers.

The case for lowercase first

The counter-argument is just as real: children see lowercase letters far more than capitals in the books being read to them, and reading instruction usually starts with lowercase sounds. Some programs — many UK primary schools and Montessori classrooms among them — teach lowercase first specifically to keep handwriting and early reading in step with each other. Neither approach is "the" correct one; both are used successfully by real programs, and the deciding factor is usually which system a school or curriculum has already committed to.

What we'd suggest, and how to practice it

If nothing else is dictating the order, start with the capital of the child's own name — it's usually already familiar from seeing it written, and it's simpler to draw than its lowercase counterpart. Our handwriting worksheet generator has a case toggle so you can build a sheet in uppercase, lowercase, or a mix, and alphabet coloring pages are a low-pressure way to build familiarity with a letter's shape before tracing it. Whichever case comes first, the next chapter covers the trickiest part of lowercase: telling b, d, p, and q apart.
