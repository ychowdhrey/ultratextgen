Cursive: When to Start and Which Letters to Teach First

When children are ready for cursive, why letters are taught in families rather than A to Z, which letters to start with, and how capitals fit in.

Handwriting · Chapter 8 of 9 Cursive ⏱ 5 min read

Key takeaways

  • Cursive usually starts after print is established — commonly around 2nd–3rd grade in US schools that teach it.
  • Teach letter families, not alphabetical order: undercurve, overcurve, oval, and loop letters share the same movements.
  • Lowercase first — it's over 90% of real writing. Capitals can wait until the lowercase alphabet flows.
  • A few letters look nothing like print (G, Q, S, z, r) — treat them as brand-new shapes, not variations.

When cursive fits

Cursive is usually introduced once print handwriting is functional — the child can write legible print sentences without exhausting concentration. In US schools that teach cursive, that's typically second or third grade, though adults learn it on the same path at any age. The prerequisite isn't age; it's that the basic pencil-control battle is already won, so attention can go to the new letterforms and joins.

Families, not alphabetical order

Cursive letters group into families that share an opening movement, and teaching by family means each new letter is mostly review. The undercurve family (i, u, w, t, r, s, j, p) shares the swing-up entry stroke — i and u are the classic first letters. The overcurve family (m, n, v, x, y, z) enters over the top. The oval family (a, c, d, g, o, q) opens with the same curve-left oval. The loop family (l, b, e, f, h, k) rises into a loop. Master one member and its siblings come cheap — which is why a family-ordered week beats an A-to-Z march. Every letter's page in our cursive alphabet names its family and links its siblings.

Four letters deserve a warning label: the cursive capital G and Q, and the lowercase s, r and z, look genuinely unlike their print forms. Teach them as new shapes — children who expect a decorated print letter get stuck; children told "this one's a brand-new letter" don't.

Lowercase first, capitals later, joins throughout

Lowercase letters make up the overwhelming majority of real writing, so they come first; capitals can wait until the lowercase alphabet flows, then arrive fastest through the child's own initials. Joins are the point of cursive, so practice pairs and short words early — in, it, at, an — rather than isolated letters only. Watch the four top-joiners (o, b, v, w), which hand over to the next letter at the midline and change its entry stroke; on and an make a revealing practice pair.

Start with the undercurve family — i and u first, then t, w, and e. They use the simplest shared entry stroke, and each additional family member is mostly review.
Lowercase first. It accounts for most of real writing, and many cursive capitals are complex, decorative forms. Introduce capitals once lowercase flows — starting with the child's initials.
