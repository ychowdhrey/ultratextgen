The Big Picture

What Unicode 18 Adds

Every year, the Unicode Consortium — the body that assigns a number to every letter, symbol, and emoji computers can exchange — ships one major update to the standard. Unicode 18.0 is that update for 2026, and as of this month its contents are effectively settled: the public beta review ran from May 26 to July 7, 2026, it has now closed, and the character repertoire is locked pending the Unicode Technical Committee's publication vote.

The headline numbers: about 13,047 new characters, taking the standard from 159,801 to 172,848 characters total. Inside that figure are four new scripts, nine new emoji, a batch of new mathematical symbols, and three new currency signs.

If you only remember one thing from this guide, make it this: a Unicode release is a catalog update, not a software update. When 18.0 ships, these characters get official numbers — but nothing changes on your phone that day. That distinction drives everything below, especially the emoji timeline.

The Catch

Why You Can't Use the New Emoji Yet — the Phone-Rollout Gap

Here is the part nearly every "new emoji announced!" article gets wrong, and the single most useful thing to understand about any Unicode release: new emoji do not appear on your phone the day Unicode ships them. Not that day, not that month — usually not for half a year or more.

The reason is a division of labor. Unicode assigns each new emoji a code point — its number in the universal catalog. But the picture you see is drawn by whoever makes your device's emoji set: Apple for iPhones, Google for Android and Gmail, Samsung for its own phones. Each vendor designs its own artwork for the new characters and ships it inside a system or keyboard update on its own schedule. For Emoji 18.0, that means devices are expected to get the new emoji around spring 2027 — months after the standard itself is finalized in 2026.

Until your device gets that update, a freshly minted emoji renders as a blank box — tofu, ▯ — or sometimes a question mark. This is the exact "why does the new emoji show as a box" confusion that spikes after every release: someone with an updated phone sends Cracked Smiling Face, and everyone on older software receives a rectangle. Nothing is broken, and nobody sent you a corrupted message. The character's number arrived; its picture hasn't. It's the same mechanism behind every tofu box on the internet — our guide on why fonts show as boxes covers the glyph layer in depth, and boxes vs. mojibake vs. question marks shows how to tell the failure modes apart.

The practical rules that follow:

  • Don't trust screenshots over your own keyboard. Articles will show vendor mockups and sample glyphs long before your phone can render the real thing.
  • Expect a lopsided window. Through late 2026 and early 2027, updated devices and platforms will see the new emoji while everyone else sees boxes — the same character, two very different experiences. (Our iPhone emoji library is a good reference for what current devices actually render.)
  • Wait for the artwork before using them anywhere permanent. A bio or username decorated with a character most of your audience can't see yet is just a row of rectangles.

Currency

New Currency Signs: Dirham, Rial, Rufiyaa

Tucked among the thousands of scholarly additions are three characters with immediate real-world weight: currency signs for the UAE dirham, the Omani rial, and the Maldivian rufiyaa.

The dirham is the headliner. The UAE recently unveiled a newly designed symbol for its currency — and Unicode 18.0 is what turns that national design into a single, universal character. Until now, one of the world's most-traded Gulf currencies has been written as "AED" or "Dhs" in every price tag, invoice, and banking app, the way the rupee was written "Rs" before ₹ arrived in Unicode. A dedicated code point is the step that eventually puts the dirham sign on keyboards, in fonts, and in financial software worldwide — the same path ₹ and ₺ took before it. For Gulf and wider MENA users, this is the quiet headline of the release.

The same rollout logic from the emoji section applies here, though: a code point is not a glyph. The dirham, rial, and rufiyaa signs will render as boxes until operating systems and fonts add them — so "AED" remains the safe spelling in anything customer-facing for a while yet. Once support lands, they'll join the full set in our currency symbols library, where every established sign already lives, copy-ready.

Scripts & Symbols

Four New Scripts and a Mountain of Ideographs

By raw character count, Unicode 18 isn't an emoji release at all — it's a historical-preservation release. The single largest addition is 11,328 ideographs from the Small Seal script, the unified writing system of China's Qin dynasty. That one block accounts for the overwhelming majority of the ~13,047 new characters, and it exists so scholars can digitize, search, and publish two-millennia-old texts as real text instead of photographs.

The release adds four new scripts in total, including Chisoi, a script from northeast India, and Jurchen, the historical script of the Jurchen people of northeastern China. This is Unicode doing its foundational job: every script added means a community — living or historical — whose writing becomes first-class data that can be typed, stored, and searched on any computer.

Rounding out the release is a fresh batch of mathematical symbols for technical publishing. Fun fact for regulars of this site: the math blocks are where most "fancy fonts" actually come from — the 𝗯𝗼𝗹𝗱 and 𝔤𝔬𝔱𝔥𝔦𝔠 alphabets you copy from our generator were added for equations, not bios.
