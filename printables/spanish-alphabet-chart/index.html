Why 27 letters, and why Ñ goes where it does

Spanish uses the same 26 letters as English, A through Z, plus one letter English doesn't have: Ñ (called eñe). Ñ isn't a decorated N — it's its own letter with its own sound, alphabetized in its own place, right between N and O: …L, M, N, Ñ, O, P…

That makes 27 letters in total, and it's why a word like español or niño is treated as a normal, complete Spanish word rather than an English one with an accent mark added on.

It used to be even more letters

Older Spanish dictionaries and alphabets list 29 or 30 letters, because Ch and Ll were once alphabetized as their own single letters — so every word starting with ch was sorted together, after every word starting with plain c, instead of mixed in alphabetically. The Real Academia Española (RAE) — the body that governs standard Spanish — dropped that rule in 1994 to match international collation standards, and its 2010 spelling reform formalized the 27-letter alphabet used today. Ch and Ll are still written exactly the same way in words; they're just sorted as two-letter combinations now, the same way English treats "ch" or "sh".
